The Unbroken Record

Two hundred and twenty-eight countries and territories reported a disease outbreak to the World Health Organization somewhere in three decades of records. Only one of them never had a year off.

01What "never stops" actually means

Set aside COVID-19's own dashboard entries, which are a separate reporting stream grafted onto this dataset from 2020 on, and the record holds 2,352 outbreak events across those 228 places. Spread evenly, a typical country would show up in most years. It doesn't work that way. The typical country in this record shows up in six years out of thirty — a single outbreak, acknowledged, and then a long silence before the next one.

A much smaller group never goes quiet. Twenty-nine countries appear in fifteen years or more — over half the span on record. They are not the places WHO visits most often by chance. They are the places that are, structurally, always somewhere in this ledger.

02The standing list

Rank every country by how many of the thirty years it shows up, and the same names keep surfacing for different reasons. DRC's record is cholera first, then Ebola, then yellow fever, then mpox, then polio — twenty different diseases altogether, a country that is simply never between outbreaks. China's record is almost one disease, repeated: identified zoonotic influenza accounts for half its appearances. Niger, Chad, and Burkina Faso share the Sahel's meningitis belt. Uganda and DRC share a filovirus corridor. The United States, the United Kingdom, and France are there for a different reason again — they're the places that keep filing on pandemic-potential flu findings that most countries don't bother reporting as a DON at all.

03The other end of the list

Sixteen countries appear in this record exactly once. Read as a list of places WHO rarely worries about, it would be a misreading. Eight of those sixteen appearances landed in a single year — 2009 — when pandemic influenza reached territories that have no other outbreak history in this dataset at all: the Isle of Man, Guernsey, Jersey, the Falkland Islands, Andorra, Monaco, Botswana, Eswatini.

What this is actually showing

That isn't sixteen separate stories about sixteen quiet countries. It's what one truly global pandemic looks like inside a ledger built from WHO alerts: the one disease that reaches almost everywhere at once, including places with no other entry in thirty years, shows up as a cluster of "one-off" countries that have nothing else in common.

    04A case study in "emerging"

    Mpox is usually described as an emerging disease, which makes 2022 sound like the year it appeared. The record says otherwise. It shows up once in 1997, in a single country. Then nothing — not one WHO report mentions it anywhere on earth for the next nineteen years. It resurfaces quietly in 2016, one or two countries a year, never more, through 2021. Then in 2022: fifty-three countries report it within twelve months.

    Countries reporting mpox (monkeypox) per year — a 19-year gap, a quiet return, then two separate spikes

    The disease didn't jump from zero to global in a single step. It had been present at low background levels, one or two reports a year, since 2016. What changed in 2022 — whether it was the virus, or the watchfulness, or both at once — isn't something an event log can separate on its own. What the record can show is that the gap closed twice: 2022 at fifty-three countries, and a second wave in 2025 at thirty-five, a recurrence the 2022 story alone didn't predict.

    "Emerging" in this record usually means "the silence just ended," not "the disease is new."

    05What the standing list is actually measuring

    Being on the chronic list is not, by itself, a measure of danger. This dataset has no case counts, no deaths, no severity scores — it is a log of when WHO issued a report, not a log of what the disease did once it arrived. A country appears on the standing list because it has three things at once: an endemic disease that returns on its own schedule, a surveillance system built to catch it, and a government that keeps filing with WHO even when nothing extraordinary is happening. A country can go thirty years without a single DON and that can mean the outbreak never came, or it can mean the reporting channel was never as active to begin with. The dataset cannot tell those two apart, and neither can this piece.

    What three decades of this record does show plainly is that it is a map of reporting geography as much as outbreak geography. The paper trail is part of the story, not just the vehicle for it.

    Methodology

    Source: WHO Disease Outbreak News index, 1996–2026, via the Humanitarian Data Exchange. One row represents one disease–country–year outbreak event, not a case or death count.

    Exclusions: COVID-19 "Coronavirus dashboard" entries (1,214 rows, 2020–2026) are removed throughout — they come from a separate ongoing reporting stream grafted onto the dataset, not individual DON bulletins, and would swamp every comparison if left in. 2026 is excluded as a partial year still being filed.

    Unit of analysis: distinct country–year presence — whether a country shows up in the record for a given year at all, not how many reports, cases, or deaths that year produced.

    Known limitation: more reporting in recent years reflects, at least in part, better surveillance and a larger WHO reporting apparatus, not necessarily more outbreaks occurring. Reported counts are reporting counts.
